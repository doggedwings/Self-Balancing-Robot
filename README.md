# Self-Balancing Robot

A two-wheeled inverted pendulum robot built around a dual-core ESP32, two NEMA 17 stepper motors, an MPU6050 IMU, and a custom 3D printed chassis. 
It holds itself upright using a cascaded PID controller running at 100 Hz, drives over Bluetooth from an Xbox controller, and has a pair of MAX7219 LED matrix "eyes" so it can look at you while it does it.

# Why I Built This

An inverted pendulum is the classic control problem: inherently unstable, and it falls over the instant your loop is wrong. That makes it a brutally honest project: there's no partial credit and no way to fake it working. 
I wanted something where the mechanical design, the electronics, and the firmware all had to be right at the same time, and where I'd have to actually understand the control theory rather than copy a tuning from a tutorial.


# How It Works

The MPU6050 reports the chassis tilt angle and angular rate. The ESP32 runs a cascaded PID loop that converts that tilt into a motor acceleration command, and the two steppers drive the wheels underneath the centre of mass to keep it from falling. 
Since it can only stay upright by continuously moving, driving it is a matter of lying to the controller — commanding a small target tilt makes it chase itself forward, and a speed differential between the two wheels turns it. An Xbox controller sends those commands over Bluetooth.

# Hardware Specs

The Brain: ESP32, balance loop and Bluetooth stack pinned to separate cores 
The Motors: 2x NEMA 17 stepper motors
The Drivers: 2x A4988 stepper drivers, microstepped, STEP pulses generated by hardware timers
The Inner Ear: MPU6050 6-axis IMU (accelerometer + gyroscope) over I2C 
Power: 8x AA pack (12 V) for motors, buck converter to 5 V for ESP32 logic — separate breadboard rails
Control: Xbox controller over Bluetooth OR phone app 
Chassis & Wheels: Custom 3D printed, Fusion 360 designed — PLA/PETG structure

# Roadmap for version 2
Custom PCB — replacing the breadboard with a 2-layer board carrying the ESP32, both A4988s, the IMU, and multi-rail power regulation. Breadboard contact resistance and floating jumpers are the current biggest source of unexplained behaviour.
Aesthetics — enclosed printed shell, hidden wiring, proper face panel for the LED eyes.
IMU upgrade — moving to a BNO055 for onboard sensor fusion instead of filtering raw MPU6050 output in firmware.
