# Self-Balancing Robot

A two wheeled robot that keeps itself upright with a PID controller running on an ESP32. Two NEMA 17 steppers, an MPU6050 for tilt, and a 3D printed chassis. You drive it from a web page over WiFi.

# Why I Built This

I wanted something I could drive around, and a normal RC car felt like the obvious version of that. A robot that has to actively work to not fall over just sitting still seemed like a better problem.

The other half was that I wanted to challenge myself with a PID loop. This one couldn't be cheated. I had to manually tune it and actually learn how the controls worked, or it just fell over.

It still drives like an RC car. It just has to solve a much harder problem to do it.


# How It Works

The MPU6050 reports the chassis tilt angle and angular rate. The ESP32 runs a cascaded PID loop that converts that tilt into a motor acceleration command, and the two steppers drive the wheels underneath the centre of mass to keep it from falling. 
Since it can only stay upright by continuously moving, driving it is a matter of lying to the controller: commanding a small target tilt makes it chase itself forward, and a speed differential between the two wheels turns it. An Xbox controller sends those commands over Bluetooth.

# Hardware Specs

The Brain: ESP32, balance loop and Bluetooth stack pinned to separate cores 
The Motors: 2x NEMA 17 stepper motors
The Drivers: 2x A4988 stepper drivers, microstepped, STEP pulses generated by hardware timers
The Inner Ear: MPU6050 6-axis IMU (accelerometer + gyroscope) over I2C 
Power: 8x AA pack (12 V) for motors, buck converter to 5 V for ESP32 logic, separate breadboard rails
Control: Xbox controller over Bluetooth OR phone app 
Chassis & Wheels: Custom 3D printed, Fusion 360 designed printed in PETG

# Engineering

1. Two PID Loops Instead of One

A single PID on tilt angle keeps the robot upright but lets it slowly roll across the room, since nothing in the loop cares about position. The fix was nesting two loops: an outer loop reads wheel speed and outputs a target lean angle, and an inner loop drives motor acceleration to hit that angle. The outer loop runs at a quarter the rate of the inner one so the two don't fight each other. Driving works by lying to the controller, since asking for a small lean makes the balance loop chase it forward. Final gains were Kp 40, Ki 0.5, Kd 2.4 with a 2.8 degree trim to correct for how the IMU sits on the frame.

2. Floating Point Crashed the Step Interrupt

Step pulses are generated by a hardware timer interrupt at 20 kHz, and the first version panicked with a LoadProhibited exception the instant that timer fired. The ESP32 doesn't save its floating point registers when entering an interrupt, so any float math inside a handler faults immediately, and the motor speed variables were floats. Converting them to integers before they reach the interrupt fixed it, since the float math still happens in normal task context where it's safe. The same handler also writes GPIO registers directly instead of calling digitalWrite, which lives in flash and isn't guaranteed to be loaded when an interrupt fires.

3. Full Step Was Shaking the Sensor

At full step the motors move in 1.8 degree jolts, and that vibration travels through the frame straight into the MPU6050. The accelerometer can't distinguish motor cogging from real tilt, so the noise entered the control loop and came back out as more motor commands, producing a jitter that no amount of tuning removed. Switching the A4988s to 1/8 microstepping smoothed the motion out and eliminated most of it. Every gain had to be rescaled by 8x afterwards, since each step now moves the robot an eighth as far.

4. Power Was Half the Problem

Motors and logic run on separate breadboard rails, because steppers pull enough current in bursts to sag a shared rail, and a sagging rail resets the ESP32 mid correction in a way that looks exactly like a firmware bug. Two failures cost real hardware along the way. One A4988 failed internally and shorted RESET to ground, which pulled the 3.3V rail down through the ESP32's onboard regulator until it cooked. And the 8x AA pack reads 11.8V with nothing attached but collapses to 2V the moment the motors draw current once the cells are spent, so open circuit voltage tells you nothing about whether a pack is still good.

# Roadmap for version 2

Custom PCB replacing the breadboard. A 2 layer board carrying the ESP32, both A4988s, the IMU, and separate regulation for each rail. Breadboard contact resistance and jumpers working loose are the biggest source of unexplained behaviour right now.

Xbox controller over Bluetooth instead of the web page, for lower latency and analog stick control.

Aesthetics. Enclosed printed shell, hidden wiring, proper face panel.

IMU upgrade to a BNO055, which does sensor fusion onboard instead of filtering raw MPU6050 output in firmware.
